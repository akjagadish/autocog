## System Prompt

You are a renowned cognitive scientist arbitrating between two theories across multiple experiments in the Decision Making (Binary Features) domain.

Each experiment was proposed alongside a metric and an expected outcome. For each experiment you are shown the design, the metric, both theories' predicted metric values (from simulated data), and the observed metric value on real data. The two theories are tagged by stable labels (e.g. "pi_5" and "pi_2") and the same labels are reused on each experiment's predictions.

Each metric value below is shown as `point_estimate (var=X)`, where `point_estimate` is `metric(data)` evaluated on the full pooled dataset and `var` is the population (between-subject) variance of the same metric re-applied per `subject_id`. The point estimate is the canonical scalar; `var` reports how consistent that estimate is across subjects (lower = more consistent). `var=n/a` means the metric could not be applied to a single-subject slice.

The goal of the arbitration is to surface theories that are task-invariant: that is, theories that can explain data across all experiments in the same domain. Perform a deep dive: which among the two theories better captures the observed data?Do not just look at the newest experiments, but look at all experiments together. If a theory is good at explaining all the data keep it. However, if the both theories are good at explaining some experiment but not all, then it might be a good idea to propose a completly new theory that can potentially explain all the data. It is often better to propose a new theory than to propose a new model. Even if one theory is clearly better than the other, instead of proposing a new model, you can propose a new theory that is a stronger competitor to the winning theory instead of proposing a new model. Only propose a new model if both theories are very good and you are confident that the new model will be better than the current one clearly distinguish the two theories.  Then issue a verdict: either "new_model" (keep the current theory description, but regenerate new predict / policy / parameter ranges such that they better capture the observed data across all experiments) or  "new_theory" (the current theory is degenerate; propose a brand-new theory that can better capture the observed data across all experiments). `target_theory_idx` is 1 if you are acting on the theory labelled "pi_5" (THEORY 1 below), or 2 if you are acting on the theory labelled "pi_2" (THEORY 2 below). Justify your choice.

## EXPERIMENTAL DOMAIN
Subjects repeatedly choose between two fictitious products, A and B. Each option is described by a vector of binary expert ratings (each 0 or 1). Every experiment fixes its own feature count (via `validities` length) and per-expert validities; both are LLM-proposed. The validities are communicated to the subject in the instructions. Subjects pick whichever product they believe is of higher quality. There is no trial-by-trial correctness feedback.

## THEORY 1 — pi_5
Salience-Weighted Tallying with Near-Tie Threshold: Decision-makers primarily rely on a non-compensatory Tallying heuristic, counting the number of features where one option dominates the other. However, if the difference in tallies is small (below a specific threshold), a secondary compensatory evaluation is triggered. In this tie-breaking phase, the weights of the features are dynamically adjusted based on their salience rather than strictly their objective validity, allowing less predictive features to become disproportionately salient and overweighted during conflict resolution.

`predict(parameters, state, history) -> np.ndarray`:
def predict(parameters, state, history):
    import numpy as np
    
    stim = np.asarray(state, dtype=float)
    if stim.ndim != 2 or stim.shape[0] != 2:
        raise ValueError(f"Expected a (2, n_features) stimulus; got shape {stim.shape}.")
        
    a, b = stim[0], stim[1]
    
    # Primary stage: Tallying feature wins
    a_wins = float(np.sum(a > b))
    b_wins = float(np.sum(b > a))
    
    tally_diff = np.abs(a_wins - b_wins)
    tally_threshold = float(parameters["tally_threshold"])
    
    if tally_diff > tally_threshold:
        scores = np.array([a_wins, b_wins])
    else:
        # Secondary stage: Salience-weighted tie-breaker
        val = np.asarray(parameters["validities"], dtype=float)
        gamma = float(parameters["gamma"])
        centered_val = val - 0.5
        
        # Non-linear scaling allows for negative gamma (overweighting less valid cues)
        w = np.sign(centered_val) * ((np.abs(centered_val) + 1e-4) ** gamma)
        
        tie_scale = float(parameters["tie_scale"])
        scores = tie_scale * np.array([np.sum(a * w), np.sum(b * w)])
        
    beta = float(parameters["beta"])
    epsilon = float(parameters["epsilon"])
    
    # Softmax with max-subtraction for numerical stability
    z = beta * (scores - np.max(scores))
    e = np.exp(z)
    p_core = e / np.sum(e)
    
    n_opts = p_core.shape[0]
    return (1.0 - epsilon) * p_core + epsilon * (np.ones(n_opts) / n_opts)


`policy(probs) -> int`:
def policy(probabilities):
    import numpy as np
    probabilities = np.asarray(probabilities, dtype=np.float64)
    probabilities /= probabilities.sum()
    return np.random.choice(len(probabilities), p=probabilities)


## THEORY 2 — pi_2
People compare two options by counting, across all features, how often one option has a higher value than the other. The option that wins on more features is chosen. Tallying discards cardinal magnitudes — only the sign of each feature-wise comparison matters — so the heuristic is robust to monotone rescaling of individual features and cannot be swayed by a single large feature difference in the way Equal-Weight can. Ties on an individual feature contribute nothing to either count: that cue is simply treated as uninformative for the pair. No feature is privileged, in contrast to Take The Best; every cue contributes equally to the tally. When the two counts are equal the heuristic has no basis for preference and the learner must guess. Response noise enters through a softmax over the two tallies with inverse temperature beta (interpolating between fully deterministic choice at large beta and uniform guessing at beta = 0), plus an independent lapse that with probability epsilon replaces the softmax output with a uniform pick over the two options.


`predict(parameters, state, history) -> np.ndarray`:
def predict(parameters, stimulus, history):
    # Paper-faithful Tallying heuristic (Dawes 1979; Gigerenzer &
    # Goldstein 1999). Stimulus is the pair of option feature vectors
    # for the current trial: array-like of shape (2, n_features),
    # with row 0 = option A, row 1 = option B. History is ignored.
    stim = np.asarray(stimulus, dtype=float)
    if stim.ndim != 2 or stim.shape[0] != 2:
        raise ValueError(
            f"Tallying expects a (2, n_features) stimulus; got shape {stim.shape}."
        )

    a, b = stim[0], stim[1]
    # Count strict feature-wise wins; ties contribute to neither option.
    a_wins = float(np.sum(a > b))
    b_wins = float(np.sum(b > a))
    scores = np.array([a_wins, b_wins])

    beta = float(parameters["beta"])
    epsilon = float(parameters["epsilon"])

    # Softmax with max-subtraction for numerical stability. When
    # a_wins == b_wins the softmax is exactly uniform regardless of
    # beta, which is the correct behavior for an undiscriminating
    # tally.
    z = beta * (scores - scores.max())
    e = np.exp(z)
    p_core = e / e.sum()

    n_opts = p_core.shape[0]
    return (1.0 - epsilon) * p_core + epsilon * (np.ones(n_opts) / n_opts)

    return np.random.choice(len(probabilities), p=probabilities)

**Rationale:** To effectively dissociate Salience-Weighted Tallying with a Near-Tie Threshold from Pure Tallying, we must test 'near-ties' (tally differences of 1 or 2) rather than just exact ties. Pure Tallying invariably favors the option with more feature wins, regardless of the margin or the validities of the features involved. The Advocated theory, however, posits that if the tally difference falls within a specific threshold, a secondary compensatory tie-breaker evaluates the options based on feature salience. By pitting an option that wins on fewer but highly valid (salient) features against an option that wins on more but lower-validity features, we create trials where Pure Tallying strongly predicts a win for the tally-leader, but the Advocated theory predicts a reversal in preference due to the near-tie threshold activating the salience-weighted tie-breaker.

**Computed schedule:** 8 unique pairs × 12 reps = 96 trials per subject.



### METRIC
Rationale:
Pure Tallying relies exclusively on counting feature wins. On trials where the tally difference is exactly zero, Pure Tallying has no basis for preference and predicts a 50/50 guess, resulting in an expected absolute deviation from 0.5 near 0 (modulo binomial sampling noise). The Advocated theory, however, ALWAYS triggers its salience-weighted tie-breaker on these exact ties. Depending on the subject's 'gamma' parameter, the tie-breaker will consistently and strongly favor either the option with more valid features (if gamma > 0) or the option with less valid features (if gamma < 0). By computing the absolute deviation of the choice proportion from 0.5 on these 'exact tie' trials FOR EACH SUBJECT independently, we capture this systematic preference. Pure Tallying will yield a small value (binomial noise), whereas the Advocated theory will yield a large value (close to 0.5).

def metric(data: pd.DataFrame) -> float:
    import numpy as np
    import pandas as pd
    
    def is_tie(row):
        a = np.array(row['option_a_ratings'])
        b = np.array(row['option_b_ratings'])
        return np.sum(a > b) == np.sum(b > a)
        
    ties = data[data.apply(is_tie, axis=1)]
    if len(ties) == 0:
        return 0.0
        
    devs = []
    for subj, subj_df in ties.groupby('subject_id'):
        p_A = np.mean(subj_df['response'] == 0)
        devs.append(np.abs(p_A - 0.5))
        
    return float(np.mean(devs))

**Rationale:** To dissociate Pure Tallying (Advocated Theory) from Salience-Weighted Tallying with a Near-Tie Threshold (Competing Theory), we focus on 'near-tie' trials where the tally difference is exactly 1 or 2. Pure Tallying strictly follows the tally count, predicting a consistent preference for the option with more feature wins, regardless of the validities of those features. The Competing Theory posits that small tally differences fall under a threshold, triggering a salience-weighted compensatory tie-breaker. By pitting an option with fewer but highly valid feature wins against an option with more but lower-validity feature wins, Pure Tallying predicts the tally-leader will win, whereas the Competing Theory predicts a preference reversal towards the option with fewer, more salient features. We also include exact ties to contrast Pure Tallying's prediction of 50/50 guessing against the Competing Theory's deterministic choice.

**Computed schedule:** 6 unique pairs × 16 reps = 96 trials per subject.



### METRIC
Rationale:
Pure Tallying treats exact ties in tally as a complete guess, predicting a 50/50 choice probability regardless of the features' validities. This means a subject's empirical choice proportion on exact tie trials will be close to 0.5, with small binomial variance. In contrast, the Competing Theory assumes that a tally difference of 0 always triggers a compensatory tie-breaker. Because the tie-breaker weights features by salience, it will strongly favor one option over the other (which one depends on the subject's gamma parameter). Consequently, the Competing Theory predicts deterministic or near-deterministic choices on these trials, leading to an empirical choice proportion far from 0.5. By computing the absolute deviation of each subject's choice proportion from 0.5 on exact tie trials, we expect a value near 0.1 for Pure Tallying and > 0.3 for the Competing Theory, highly discriminating the two.
